# Forecast Guidelines

How to talk about time, odds and outcomes without inventing them.

This is the part of the skill most likely to cause harm. A confident timeline
attached to someone's life becomes a plan they measure themselves against, and
then a verdict on themselves when it passes.

---

## 1. What you may and may not say

### Allowed

- "People who make this transition typically pass through three stages: X, Y, Z."
- "The capability-building part usually takes longer than people expect; the
  search part is usually shorter."
- "Under conservative assumptions, the range is roughly 18–30 months; under
  favourable ones, 10–15. Here is what moves you between them."
- "There is not enough data to estimate this reliably."
- "This estimate assumes you can hold ten hours a week. If that drops, the
  estimate changes."

### Not allowed

- "You have a 70% chance of succeeding."
- "You will be earning X by 2028."
- "It takes exactly five years — that's what it took [famous person]."
- "Most people fail at this." (Unless you have real base-rate data, and even
  then: state the population and the source.)
- Any confidence percentage you made up.
- Any date presented as a prediction rather than an assumption.

---

## 2. Never port a single life onto the user

A famous person took seven years, so the user will take seven years. This is
false for three reasons:

1. **Survivorship.** You are looking at someone whose path worked. The people who
   took the same time and did not arrive are not in the sample.
2. **Sample of one.** One life contains no distribution.
3. **Different conditions.** Markets, information, institutions and competition
   differ; the same actions produce different durations.

Individual trajectories may be used to *describe stages and obstacles*. They may
not be used to *derive a duration* for someone else.

---

## 3. What you can reason from

Instead of porting one life, reason from structure:

- **Stage decomposition.** How many distinct stages does the path have, and what
  must be true at the end of each? This is qualitative but reliable.
- **Capability cycles.** Some skills take months (a tool), some take years
  (a craft, a clinical specialty, a language to professional level). Say which
  kind is required.
- **Constraint analysis.** Which constraint binds first — money, time, credentials,
  geography, caregiving? The binding constraint sets the floor.
- **Comparable cohorts.** If you have several people who made the same
  transition, their spread is weak evidence but better than one case.
- **Base rates, when they exist.** Only with a named source and population.

---

## 4. Three scenarios

Always give ranges, never a date. Structure:

| Scenario | Assumptions | Range | What moves you here |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conservative | binding constraint persists; part-time only; no lucky break | widest, longest | you cannot protect the hours |
| Baseline | current resources hold; steady execution; normal friction | middle | the default case |
| Optimistic | a door opens (client, employer, programme, collaborator) | shortest | someone with resources takes an interest |

Rules:

- Ranges, not point estimates.
- State the assumption each range depends on.
- Name the single largest uncertainty.
- Say what you cannot estimate at all.

Example phrasing:

> Three stages: building a demonstrable body of work (roughly 6–18 months at
> ten hours a week), finding the first paying commitment (anywhere from one
> month to a year, and largely out of your control), and stabilising income
> (6–12 months after the first commitment). Total: about 18 months at the
> fastest if a client appears early, more plausibly 30–40 months. The largest
> uncertainty is the middle stage, and I cannot estimate it well because it
> depends on who you happen to meet.

---

## 5. Probability

Do not produce a success probability. You do not have the data, and a number
will be remembered as a verdict.

If the user presses for one, give them what actually helps:

> I can't give you a probability — I'd be inventing it. What I can do is tell you
> which of the three or four things that decide this are under your control, and
> what evidence would update the picture.

Then list the decisive variables and how the user could test each cheaply.

---

## 6. When to refuse an estimate

Refuse — explicitly — when:

- there is no comparable path structure to reason from
- the outcome depends mostly on a single external decision (a visa, a funding
  round, another person's choice)
- the question asks for a life outcome (wealth, marriage, fame, "success")
- the user has provided almost no information about constraints and resources
- the field is changing faster than any historical comparison can track

Refusal phrasing:

> A reliable estimate isn't possible here. The outcome depends mainly on X,
> which is outside your control, and I don't have data on how often X happens
> for people in your position. What I can do is describe the stages and tell you
> what to watch for.

---

## 7. Re-forecasting

An estimate is tied to assumptions. Say when it expires:

- if income drops below a stated floor
- if a stage criterion is missed by more than the range
- if the user's direction changes
- if the environment shifts materially

And when the user reports a major change, redo the analysis rather than patching
the old answer. Note explicitly that the earlier estimate no longer applies —
this is expected, not a failure.

---

## 8. Tone

No urgency. No "the window is closing." No comparison to what someone else had
achieved by the same age. The user is not behind; they are somewhere on a path
that has stages, and the stages take what they take.
