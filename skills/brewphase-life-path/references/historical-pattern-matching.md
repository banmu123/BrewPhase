# Historical Pattern Matching

The research procedure behind every substantive answer. Five steps, in order.
Each step has a failure mode that the next step cannot repair.

---

## Step 1 — Read the user before searching

Establish these nine things. Mark anything you do not actually know as unknown;
do not fill it in with a plausible guess.

1. **Current stage** — starting out, mid-career, between roles, recovering from failure, already building something
2. **Growth background** — family circumstances, education available, early environment (only what they said)
3. **Skills and resources** — what they can already do, who they know, what capital and time they have
4. **Work and life history** — the shape of what they have done, not just the last job title
5. **The real obstacle** — the thing that actually stops them, which is often not the thing they named first
6. **The desired state** — what they want to be true, in their own words
7. **Hard constraints** — money, caregiving, health, geography, legal status, age limits
8. **Already tried** — what did not work, and why they think it did not
9. **Open questions** — what they have not decided and are not ready to decide

Ask at most two or three questions, and only about things that change the
analysis. If the user cannot answer, proceed and state the gap.

**Failure mode:** you invent a tidy backstory and then research a person who does
not exist.

---

## Step 2 — Abstract into an anonymous pattern

Never search with a person. Search with a shape.

Write one paragraph containing only these eleven dimensions:

| Dimension | Prompt |
|---|---|
| Starting point | What did they begin with, materially and culturally? |
| Knowledge source | Formal education, apprenticeship, self-teaching, institutions? |
| Resources | Capital, network, time, family support, institutional backing? |
| Capability and interest | What are they actually good at, and what pulls them? |
| Core goal | What state are they trying to reach? |
| Key constraint | What blocks it, and is it external or internal? |
| Decision style | Deliberate, opportunistic, risk-averse, impulsive? |
| Response to failure | Rebuild, retreat, reframe, repeat? |
| Stage of development | Exploring, building, consolidating, transitioning, declining? |
| Environment | Market, institution, era, region, family situation? |
| Turning point | What choice or event would change everything? |

Then compress it into a **searchable anonymous pattern**. Example shape:

> A self-taught technical practitioner in a mid-sized city, with stable but
> modest income and limited capital, wanting to move from employment into
> independent work in a field where credentials matter to buyers, with roughly
> ten hours a week and no safety net.

This paragraph — not the user's story — is what goes into a search box.
Strip names, employers, cities, ages and any combination that could identify
them.

**Failure mode:** you search "how did [famous founder] start" and get a
hagiography, because that is what the query asks for.

---

## Step 3 — Retrieve widely

### Quantity

For a full path analysis, study **4–6 people**. Not one, not twelve.

Hard coverage floor:

- ≥ 2 historical or earlier-period figures
- ≥ 2 modern or contemporary figures
- ≥ 3 distinct cultural or geographic backgrounds
- ≥ 3 distinct career or path types

If the question is genuinely narrow, reduce the count and **say why**:
"Because you asked specifically about research careers in Japan, I studied three
Japanese scientists rather than a global set."

### Query construction

Search for the trajectory, not the triumph. Useful query families:

- `"<person> early career before"` / `"<person> 早年 经历"`
- `"<person> failure"` / `"<person> setback"` / `"<person> 失败 挫折"`
- `"<person> biography"` + `"critical"` or `"scholarly"`
- `"<person> interview"` + `"how did you"` / `"what was hard"`
- `"<field> <era> <region> career path"` — to find people you have not heard of
- `"<institution> alumni"` / `"<lab> former members"` — to find a cohort
- Non-English equivalents: `trayectoria`, `مسيرة`, `経歴`, `percurso`, `जीवनी`

Search in at least two languages when the case is outside the anglophone world.

### What to look for

- How they actually spent their twenties and thirties, year by year if possible
- The job that paid the bills while the real work happened
- The specific turning decision, and what it cost
- What they were bad at, and what they never fixed
- Who opened which door, and why
- What they gave up: health, family, money, reputation, time
- Their own account, in their own words, with its self-serving bias intact
- Later scholars' reconstructions and disagreements

### What not to accept as research

- Listicles ("10 habits of successful people")
- Motivational summaries and quote pages
- Corporate biographies and PR profiles
- AI-generated biography sites with no named author
- Anything that presents a single cause for a complex life

**Failure mode:** you retrieve six famous founders from one country and call it a
global comparison.

## Step 4 — Verify

Grade every source (see `source-evaluation.md`) and cross-check the facts that
carry weight: dates, sequence, who did what first, whether a venture actually
succeeded, whether a degree was completed.

Minimum standard for a consequential claim: **two independent reliable sources**,
or one primary source plus one scholarly treatment.

Then label each statement:

- **Fact** — documented, cross-checked
- **Their account** — the person's own recollection or framing
- **Scholarly view** — a researcher's interpretation
- **Your inference** — reasoning you did, clearly marked

Where sources conflict, present the conflict rather than picking a side
silently:

> Accounts differ on whether he left voluntarily or was pushed out; his own
> memoir says the former, two colleagues' interviews suggest the latter. The
> difference matters because it changes whether the move was a choice or a
> constraint.

**Failure mode:** a vivid anecdote from one popular article becomes the
foundation of the whole comparison.

---

## Step 5 — Build the dossier

For each person, fill all eighteen fields. If a field cannot be filled, write
"not established" — an honest gap is information.

1. Name
2. Period
3. Geographic and cultural context
4. Domain
5. Starting point and formative environment
6. Education and capability accumulation
7. Major life stages (with rough ages or years)
8. Key decisions
9. Difficulties faced
10. Failures and setbacks
11. Outcomes achieved
12. Conditions that made the outcome possible
13. Costs paid
14. Similarities to the user
15. Differences from the user
16. What is transferable
17. What must not be copied
18. Sources and confidence level

Fields 12, 13 and 17 are the ones that make the dossier useful and the ones most
often skipped. A trajectory without its enabling conditions and its price is a
story, not evidence.

**Failure mode:** you produce fields 1, 2, 4 and 11 — the parts that read well —
and skip everything that would let the user act.

## Anti-patterns

Named so you can catch yourself doing them:

| Anti-pattern | Symptom |
|---|---|
| The pantheon | Same five famous people every time |
| The moral | Every biography ends with the same lesson about persistence |
| Survivorship | Only people who succeeded; nobody who tried and stopped |
| Flattening | "He failed many times, then succeeded" with no specifics |
| Anachronism | Applying today's market conditions to a 19th-century career |
| Transfer by vibe | "You're similar, so do what they did" |
| Single-source certainty | One article, stated as established history |
| Quota fabrication | Inventing a case to satisfy a regional coverage rule |

## Worked shape (abbreviated)

Input: a self-taught developer in a mid-sized city, stable income, wants to go
independent, has ten hours a week, no savings buffer.

Abstracted pattern: *technically competent, credential-light, capital-poor,
time-poor, needs buyer trust, cannot absorb a long unpaid runway.*

Retrieval: one 19th-century craftsman-turned-manufacturer (capital-poor start),
one mid-20th-century engineer who consulted before founding (same trust problem),
one contemporary solo practitioner in a trust-sensitive field (same constraint,
current conditions), one person who tried and returned to employment (the
counter-case), one person from a different region entirely to test whether the
pattern is culture-specific.

Then: dossier each, compare on constraint shape, and only then propose routes.
Note that none of the five is chosen for fame, and that the counter-case carries
as much information as the successes.
