# Path Planning

Turning research into two or three routes the user could actually take, and one
to three actions worth doing now.

A path is not a plan. A plan assumes the future cooperates; a path says "if you
go this way, here is what it demands, what it risks, and what would tell you to
turn back."

---

## 1. Ten elements of a complete path analysis

Every full analysis answers all ten. Short answers may answer fewer, but never
skip 7 and 9.

1. **Current state** — where the user actually is, in concrete terms
2. **Long-term direction** — the state they want, in their words
3. **Current stage goal** — the one thing this stretch is for
4. **Verification criteria** — how they would know the stage is done, observably
5. **Possible paths** — 2–3 distinct routes
6. **Required capabilities and resources** — per path, including what they lack
7. **Principal obstacles** — per path, named specifically
8. **Next action** — the single most useful thing to do in the next few weeks
9. **Deliberately not doing** — what to stop or postpone, and why
10. **Re-evaluation conditions** — what would make this analysis wrong

Element 4 protects the user from a stage that can never end. Element 9 protects
them from doing everything at once. Element 10 protects them from a plan that
outlives its premises.

---

## 2. Three path archetypes

Offer 2–3, drawn from these, chosen to fit the user's actual constraints — not
one of each by default.

### A. Steady accumulation

Keep income, build capability on the side, move when the side work can carry the
transition.

- **Fits:** no financial buffer, caregiving obligations, visa or age constraints,
  a field where credibility accrues slowly
- **Demands:** sustained part-time effort over a long period; tolerance for slow
  visible progress
- **Risks:** the side work never grows enough to become primary; the long middle
  period erodes motivation; opportunity cost accumulates silently
- **Fails when:** the person cannot protect the time, or the field requires
  full-time presence to compete

### B. High-autonomy exploration

Deliberately reduce commitments, take varied work, optimise for learning rate and
option value over income stability.

- **Fits:** low fixed costs, no dependants, youth or a genuine reset point, a
  field where portfolio and range matter more than tenure
- **Demands:** real tolerance for income volatility; a way to signal competence
  without a linear CV
- **Risks:** drift without a decision rule; shallow breadth; the runway ends
  before anything compounds
- **Fails when:** there is no defined checkpoint or stopping condition

### C. Cross-domain transition

Carry an existing capability into a different field, trading domain knowledge for
a new context.

- **Fits:** a real, transferable skill; a target field that values outsiders;
  willingness to start at a lower status
- **Demands:** translating the old capability into the new field's language;
  accepting a temporary drop in seniority
- **Risks:** being read as unqualified in both domains; the old field's identity
  is hard to leave
- **Fails when:** the transfer is asserted rather than demonstrated with work

A fourth archetype exists and should be named when relevant: **stay and
renegotiate** — change the conditions of the current path rather than the path.
It is underrated and often correct.

## 3. Making paths concrete

For each path, specify:

```
Path name:
  What you would do, in the first 90 days
  What it requires that you do not have yet
  The main obstacle, named
  What it costs (money, time, status, relationships)
  Early signal that it is working
  Early signal that it is not
  Who has done something structurally similar (from the research)
```

The last line is where the historical work pays off — but reference the
*constraint match*, not the person's fame.

---

## 4. Sequencing

Order matters more than selection. Common sequencing errors:

- Building the product before knowing who buys it
- Leaving the job before the substitute income exists, with no runway
- Studying for a credential that the target field does not actually require
- Going full-time on something that has never been tested part-time
- Optimising a skill the market does not pay for, because it is comfortable

When two paths are both viable, prefer the one whose first step is **cheapest to
reverse**. Reversibility is worth more than speed for someone with limited
runway.

---

## 5. Next actions

Give **1–3**, not ten. Each must be:

- **Specific** — an action, not a theme ("write to three people who have made
  this transition", not "build your network")
- **Small enough to start this week**
- **Diagnostic** — it should produce information, not just activity
- **Reversible** where possible
- **Tied to a stage criterion** — doing it should move the verification test

Prefer actions that test the riskiest assumption. If the whole path depends on
"people will pay for this", the next action is a paid commitment from a real
person, not a business plan.

---

## 6. Deliberately not doing

Name 2–4 things to stop or postpone, with a reason and a re-entry condition.

Examples:

- "Do not start the second product line yet — it splits the only scarce resource
  you have, which is focused weeks."
- "Postpone the master's degree. Nothing in your target field requires it, and
  it costs two years of the runway you need."
- "Stop rewriting the portfolio. It is not the bottleneck; the absence of
  conversations with buyers is."

Never phrase this as failure. The spec is explicit: something being unimportant
now does not make it a failure, and it should not be deleted permanently.

---

## 7. Re-evaluation conditions

State what would invalidate the analysis. Concrete triggers:

- income drops below a stated floor
- a stage criterion is not met within its range
- the direction itself changes (the user no longer wants the state they named)
- the environment shifts materially (market, regulation, family situation)
- a key assumption is disproved by the first actions

Without these, a plan becomes a commitment, and the user keeps following a path
that stopped fitting months ago.

---

## 8. What not to produce

- A 12-month week-by-week plan. It will be wrong by week three.
- A list of ten goals. It is a wish list, not a path.
- A single path presented as the answer. The user needs to choose.
- A path that requires resources the user does not have, without saying so.
- Anything that reads as "if you just work hard enough."
