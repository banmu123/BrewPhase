# Personal Analysis — Matching a Person to a Path

How to compare the user with a historical figure without turning a life into a
similarity score.

The rule that governs this whole file: **a match is not a resemblance, it is a
set of transferable constraints.** Two people can look nothing alike and share
the exact constraint that matters — and two people can look alike and share
nothing that transfers.

---

## 1. Eight dimensions

Compare on these, one at a time. For each, record a verdict: **similar**,
**partly similar**, **different**, or **unknown**.

| # | Dimension | The question to actually answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Starting point | Did they begin from a comparable position in resources, status and family circumstance? |
| 2 | Environment | Is the surrounding economy, institution and culture comparable in what it permits? |
| 3 | Resources | Capital, network, time, credentials, sponsorship — comparable or not? |
| 4 | Capability accumulation | How did they get good? Same route as the user's available route, or a different one? |
| 5 | Problem shape | Is the obstacle structurally the same (trust, capital, credentials, caregiving, age, geography)? |
| 6 | Goal | Is the desired end state comparable in kind, not just in ambition? |
| 7 | Key decisions | Are the choices available to them actually available to the user? |
| 8 | Development stage | Are they at a comparable point in the arc, or has the user compared themselves to someone twenty years ahead? |

Dimension 5 is the one that carries most of the analytical weight. Dimension 8 is
the one most often skipped, and the one that produces most of the user's
unnecessary anxiety.

---

## 2. Transferability, not similarity

For each figure, sort the findings into three buckets. This is the actual output
of the comparison.

### Transferable

Things that work regardless of era and country:

- how they sequenced a transition while keeping income
- how they built credibility before they had credentials
- how they used a narrow niche to enter a field
- how they handled a long period without recognition
- what they deliberately stopped doing

### Transferable with modification

Things whose *principle* holds but whose *form* does not:

- apprenticeship → today's substitutes: open-source contribution, paid
  mentorship, working inside a small team
- patron sponsorship → today's equivalents: grants, platform revenue, a single
  anchor client
- national institutional backing → today's equivalents: industry programmes,
  university labs, incubators

Say what the modern substitute is, and say what is lost in the translation.

### Not transferable

- specific market timing that has passed
- a family fortune, a personal network, a citizenship advantage
- an unusual physical or cognitive endowment
- a regulatory or institutional opening that no longer exists
- luck: being in the right place, meeting the right person at the right moment

Name these plainly. The point of the analysis is partly to *stop* the user from
copying them.

---

## 3. Phrases that are banned, and what to say instead

Never:

- "You are the next Steve Jobs."
- "You and X are 80% similar."
- "X succeeded, so if you follow the same steps you will too."
- "People like you usually…"
- "At your age, X had already…"

Instead:

> You share three things with this person: a late start, no formal credential in
> the field, and income that had to keep coming in during the transition. What
> does not transfer is the market: their field was opening up while yours is
> consolidating, and they had a sibling who could support them for two years.
> So the useful part is their sequencing — how they kept paid work while
> building credibility — not their decision to go all in.

The structure is always: **shared constraint → what it enabled → what does not
transfer → what to take.**

---

## 4. Handling the user's own framing

Users often arrive with a comparison already in mind: "I want to be like X."

What to do:

1. Take it seriously — there is usually a reason that person resonates.
2. Ask what specifically draws them: the outcome, the work, the recognition, or
   the freedom? These lead to very different research.
3. Check whether the admired person's *actual* life contains what the user wants.
   Frequently it does not: the admired founder worked eighty-hour weeks for a
   decade, and the user wants autonomy.
4. If the real desire is autonomy, research people who achieved autonomy, not
   necessarily famous founders.

---

## 5. The counter-case is mandatory

Every comparison set must include **at least one person who tried and did not
reach the goal**, or who reached it and paid a price they would not repeat.

Why: a set of successes teaches the user that the only variable is effort. A
counter-case teaches them what actually predicts failure. It is usually the most
useful part of the analysis and the part users remember.

If you cannot find a documented counter-case, say so, and use the closest thing:
someone who exited the field, someone who publicly regretted the route, or
research on base rates for that transition.

## 7. Confidence

Say how confident the comparison is, and why:

- **High** — matched on problem shape and resources, with well-documented lives
- **Medium** — matched on problem shape, but the environment differs materially
- **Low** — matched mainly on ambition; little else lines up

Low confidence is a legitimate result. Report it rather than inflating a weak
match into a strong one, and say what would raise it.
