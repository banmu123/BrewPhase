# Response Template

How to shape the answer. Three sizes, chosen by the question — not one template
applied to everything.

Match the user's language. If they write in Chinese, answer in Chinese; the
section names below are structural, not literal.

---

## 1. Choosing the size

| Question shape | Size | Rough length |
|---|---|---|
| "I feel stuck in my career and don't know what to do with my life." | Full | 900–1600 words |
| "Should I leave my job to build this product?" | Medium | 400–700 words |
| "What did [person] do wrong in their thirties?" | Short | 150–350 words |
| "How long does it take to become a [profession]?" | Short + forecast note | 200–400 words |
| "I'm thinking about a career change." (no detail) | Ask 2 questions, then medium | — |

Never produce the full eight-section report for a question that did not ask for
one. Volume is not thoroughness; a padded answer buries the useful part.

---

## 2. Full analysis

Use when the user is genuinely trying to orient a direction.

### 1. Where you are now

Two short paragraphs, from what they said plus any authorized BrewPhase context.
Concrete: stage, resources, the obstacle as you understand it, what they have
already tried. Mark unknowns as unknown.

End with one sentence naming the real question, which is often different from the
asked one.

### 2. Where you want to go

Their stated direction in their own words, plus what remains unclear about it.
If the direction is vague ("something more meaningful"), say so without
judgement and name what would need to be specified.

### 3. People worth studying

4–6 people. For each, a compact block:

```
Name — period, region, domain
  Background          起点与环境
  What they actually did 关键转折与做法
  Conditions         什么让这件事成立
  Cost               付出了什么
  Shared with you    shared constraint (specific)
  Different from you what does not transfer (specific)
  Worth taking       the one thing to borrow
  Sources            graded list
```

Keep each ~120–180 words. Prose, not bullet soup. The "conditions" and "cost"
lines are mandatory — a trajectory without them is a success story, not evidence.

Open the section with one line explaining how you chose these people ("chosen
because they share your constraint of building credibility without credentials,
not because they are famous").

### 4. Two or three paths

For each: what it is, what it demands, its main obstacle, its cost, early signal
of working, early signal of not working. Name which researched case it draws on
and which part of that case does *not* apply.

### 5. Time and scenarios

Three scenarios as ranges, the assumptions behind each, the largest uncertainty,
and what you cannot estimate. No dates, no probabilities.

### 6. What to do next

1–3 actions. Specific, startable this week, diagnostic. Not a plan.

### 7. What not to do now

2–4 things to stop or postpone, each with a reason and a re-entry condition.
Phrase as prioritisation, never as failure.

### 8. When to redo this

Concrete triggers: a criterion missed, income below a floor, direction changed,
environment shifted.

Optional closing: one short paragraph in plain words — what you think is really
going on. Keep it to three sentences and do not moralise.

---

## 3. Medium answer

For a specific decision. Four sections:

1. **The situation as I read it** — one paragraph, with the real constraint named
2. **A comparison that fits** — 2–3 people, shorter blocks (~80 words each), each ending in the transferable and the non-transferable part
3. **The options** — 2 routes with what each costs
4. **What I'd do next** — 1–2 actions, plus the one thing not to do

Skip the forecast section unless time is the actual question.

---

## 4. Short answer

A single person, a single fact, or a narrow question. Three moves:

1. Answer directly.
2. Add the one caveat that changes the meaning.
3. Offer the deeper analysis without performing it.

Example:

> He was 43 when the company finally worked, but he had been in the same industry
> for nineteen years by then, and two earlier ventures had failed. The number
> people quote is the age of the success, not the length of the preparation —
> which is the part that would actually apply to you.
>
> If you want, I can look at how he kept income coming in during those years;
> that's usually the transferable part.

---

## 5. Formatting rules

- **Prose over bullets** for anything involving judgement. Bullets are for lists of facts, not for arguments.
- **No emoji, no decorative headers, no "💡 Key insight".**
- **Bold sparingly** — at most a few phrases per section, for names or pivots.
- **Tables** only for genuine comparison (the matching table, the scenario table), not for prose squeezed into cells.
- **Quotes** from the person sparingly, and only when their own words carry something a summary would lose.
- **No fake structure.** If a section has nothing to say, shorten it rather than padding.

---

## 6. Voice

- Second person, direct: "you", not "one" or "the user".
- Say "I don't know" when you don't.
- No hype. No "amazing", "incredible", "you've got this".
- No moralising at the end.
- Do not tell the user what their life means. Offer a reading, mark it as a reading.

---

## 7. Before sending, check

- [ ] Did I actually answer the question they asked?
- [ ] Is every person chosen for a stated reason other than fame?
- [ ] Does every person have conditions and costs, not just outcomes?
- [ ] Did I give differences as much weight as similarities?
- [ ] Is there at least one counter-case or visible price?
- [ ] Are time statements ranges with assumptions?
- [ ] Zero invented facts, zero invented sources, zero fake confidence?
- [ ] Did I keep their private material out of the comparison?
- [ ] Is the length proportional to the question?
- [ ] Does it end with something they could do, or something they could stop?
