# Coverage Map

A retrieval entry point and a coverage checklist. **Not a database, not a
ranking, and not a shortlist to pick from.**

The names below are anchors: places to start looking and reminders that the
world is wider than the English-language tech press. Every analysis must go
beyond this page. If your six chosen figures all appear here, you probably
searched the list instead of the pattern.

---

## 1. Three time bands

Assign each person you study to one band, and make sure a full analysis does not
sit entirely inside one band.

| Band | Range | What it lets you examine |
|---|---|---|
| Historical | Ancient world to roughly the 18th century | How people built capability when institutions, mobility and information were far more constrained than today |
| Modern | 19th century to mid-20th century | Professionalisation, industrialisation, mass education, the birth of new fields |
| Contemporary | Late 20th century to now, including living people | Current markets, platforms, credential inflation, fast-changing technology |

Two cautions:

- **Do not impose European periodisation on the rest of the world.** "Medieval"
  and "early modern" do not map cleanly onto Chinese, Islamic, South Asian,
  African or pre-Columbian American history. Describe the period by what was
  actually happening in that society.
- **Living figures need freshness checks.** Their situation may have changed
  since the source was written. Prefer the most recent reliable material and
  date it explicitly.

## 2. Ten regions

For a global question, do not default to the US, UK and Western Europe. Actively
look for cases outside the anglophone world.

1. **China and East Asia** — China, Japan, Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Mongolia
2. **Southeast Asia** — Vietnam, Thailand, Indonesia, Malaysia, Philippines, Singapore, Myanmar, Cambodia, Laos, Brunei, Timor-Leste
3. **South Asia** — India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, Afghanistan
4. **Central and West Asia** — Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, Caucasus states
5. **Middle East** — Iran, Turkey, Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, Israel, Palestine, Jordan, Saudi Arabia, Gulf states, Yemen
6. **Africa** — North, West, East, Central and Southern Africa, including Madagascar
7. **Europe** — Western, Northern, Southern, Central and Eastern Europe, including the Balkans and the Baltic states
8. **North America** — United States, Canada, Mexico (Mexico is also legitimately Latin American; place it where it serves the question)
9. **Latin America and the Caribbean** — Brazil, Argentina, Colombia, Chile, Peru, Venezuela, Cuba, Haiti, Jamaica, and the wider region
10. **Oceania** — Australia, Aotearoa New Zealand, Papua New Guinea, Pacific island states

Language matters. When possible read in the person's own language or in a
reliable translation — Chinese, English, Arabic, Spanish, Portuguese, French,
Japanese, Hindi, Russian and so on. A case filtered entirely through English
media usually loses the constraints that made it hard.

If reliable material for a region is genuinely thin, **say so**. Never invent a
case to fill a cell in a coverage table.

## 3. Seven domains

Success is not one thing. Make sure your selection is not all founders.

1. **Business and entrepreneurship** — founders, operators, family-business builders, solo independents, social enterprise
2. **Science, technology and engineering** — researchers, engineers, inventors, medical scientists, builders of technical systems
3. **Arts and culture** — musicians, filmmakers, actors, designers, visual artists, writers
4. **Sport and competition** — athletes, coaches, sports operators, people with long competitive careers
5. **Education, scholarship and thought** — teachers, academics, philosophers, historians, public thinkers
6. **Social reform and public service** — reformers, public servants, organisers, human-rights and civic actors
7. **Exploration, invention and long professional practice** — explorers, navigators, craftspeople, specialists with decades of practice

The last domain exists because a great deal of real capability is built by
people who never became famous and never founded anything.

## 5. Getting past the anchors

Deliberately search *outside* this page. Useful moves:

- **Search by domain, not by fame.** Instead of "successful entrepreneur", try
  "founder of a machine-tool company in 1950s Osaka" or "public health doctor
  in post-independence West Africa."
- **Search in another language.** Query in Spanish, Arabic, Chinese, Japanese,
  Portuguese, Hindi or Swahili for the same pattern. You will find different
  people.
- **Follow institutions.** Ask what school, laboratory, firm or movement
  produced people who did this kind of work, then look up who passed through.
- **Look for mid-level practitioners.** Not everyone useful is canonical; a
  competent second-tier figure often transfers better than a titan.
- **Ask about women, minorities and colonised subjects explicitly.** Default
  retrieval skews heavily otherwise, and the available constraints are often the
  ones the user actually faces.

## 6. Coverage self-check

Before you write the comparison, score your own selection. If any line fails,
either fix it or say why the exception is justified.

- [ ] At least two people from historical or earlier periods
- [ ] At least two modern or contemporary people
- [ ] At least three distinct cultural or geographic backgrounds
- [ ] At least three distinct career or path types
- [ ] Not all from one country, and not all from the anglophone world
- [ ] Not all from one domain (not four founders)
- [ ] At least one person whose path *failed* or who paid a visible price
- [ ] At least one person whose starting resources resemble the user's, not just their ambition
- [ ] Every chosen person has a stated reason for selection beyond fame
- [ ] Every claim about a person carries a source grade

If the question is narrow — "how did scientists in one country build research
careers" — a reduced selection is correct. **Say that you narrowed it and why.**

---

## 7. When material is thin

For some regions, periods and ordinary lives, verifiable sources are scarce.
What to do:

1. Say it: "Reliable material on this is limited to X, and I could not verify Y."
2. Widen the search to a comparable context rather than inventing detail.
3. Prefer depth on three well-documented cases over breadth across six
   half-documented ones.
4. Never fabricate a person, an event, a date or a source to satisfy a quota.
