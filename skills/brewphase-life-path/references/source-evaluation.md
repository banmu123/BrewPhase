# Source Evaluation

How to decide whether a claim about a person's life is good enough to build an
argument on.

The failure this file prevents: a confident answer assembled from popular
summaries, where the story is right in outline and wrong in every detail that
mattered.

---

## 1. Four tiers

Grade every source before using it. When you cite, carry the grade.

### Tier A — primary and authoritative

- Autobiographies, memoirs, published diaries, collected letters
- Speeches, interviews and testimony in the person's own voice
- Official archives: state records, institutional records, court documents
- Catalogues and holdings of universities, museums, national libraries, historical institutes
- Contemporaneous documents: contracts, patents, manuscripts, meeting minutes

Strength: closest to what actually happened. Weakness: self-serving framing,
selective memory, and silence about failure.

### Tier B — scholarly

- Peer-reviewed articles and academic monographs
- Professional historical research with footnotes
- Biographies from established publishers with a named author and a source apparatus
- Edited scholarly editions and critical assessments

Strength: checked, contextualised, willing to record uncertainty. Weakness:
interpretive, sometimes contested between schools.

### Tier C — edited and fact-checked journalism

- Major outlets with editorial standards and corrections policies
- Long-form interviews with a named interviewer
- Documented investigative reporting
- Institutional profiles that name their sources

Strength: recent and specific. Weakness: framing pressure, access journalism,
and the tendency to smooth over ambiguity.

### Not a tier — entry points only

- Encyclopaedias, including Wikipedia
- Search result snippets and AI overviews
- Quote collections, "life lessons" pages, listicles
- Company biographies, press kits, personal-brand websites
- Anything without a named author or a traceable source

These are how you *find* Tier A/B/C material. They are never the final basis for
a consequential claim.

**On Wikipedia specifically:** acceptable as a map, unacceptable as a
destination. Use it to learn the shape of a life and to harvest its footnotes;
then read the footnotes. Never cite it as the source for a turning point, a
date, or a contested claim.

---

## 2. What needs two sources

Apply the double-source rule to anything that carries the argument:

- Dates and sequence of events
- Whether a venture, project or institution actually succeeded
- Whether a qualification was completed
- Who made a decision, and who merely executed it
- The reason a role ended
- Any number presented as fact (funding, sales, citations, scores)
- Any claim that contradicts the person's own account

Not everything needs this. A general characterisation ("he worked in publishing
for most of his twenties") can rest on a Tier B biography alone.

---

## 3. Label every statement

Four kinds of statement must never be blended:

| Label | Meaning | Phrasing |
|---|---|---|
| **Fact** | Documented, cross-checked | "In 1957 he moved to…" |
| **Their account** | The person's own framing | "By his own account, he…" |
| **Scholarly view** | A researcher's interpretation | "Biographers generally read this as…" |
| **Inference** | Your reasoning | "This suggests — though I can't confirm — that…" |

Mixing these is the most common way a plausible answer becomes misleading. A
memoir's version of events is evidence about how the person understood their
life, not automatically evidence about what happened.

---

## 4. When sources conflict

Do not silently choose. Do this:

1. State that accounts differ.
2. Say what each version claims and who supports it.
3. Explain why the difference matters for the user's question.
4. Say which version you find better supported, and why — or say you cannot tell.

Example:

> Two versions of the exit exist. His memoir describes a resignation on
> principle; two colleagues' later interviews describe a removal. The
> distinction matters here because the "principled exit" reading supports a
> narrative about conviction, while the removal reading suggests the constraint
> was institutional and not chosen. I weight the colleague accounts slightly
> higher because they are independent of each other and consistent, but the
> question is not settled.

---

## 5. Red flags

Treat these as reasons to slow down:

- **Too clean.** A life with no dead ends, no drift, no wasted years.
- **Single cause.** "He succeeded because he never gave up."
- **Numbers without provenance.** Funding, users, revenue with no source.
- **Recent and unanimous.** A sudden cluster of identical profiles usually
  traces to one press release or one AI-generated summary.
- **No named author, no date, no publication.** Common on biography aggregator sites.
- **Direct quotes that read like a script.** Check whether the quote appears
  identically across unrelated outlets.
- **Hagiography or hit piece.** Both flatten. Look for the source that
  acknowledges cost and also competence.
- **Anachronistic framing.** Describing a 19th-century career in the language of
  startup funding rounds.

If a claim only exists in red-flag sources, either drop it or explicitly mark it
as unverified and explain what it would take to confirm.

---

## 6. Recording citations

For each figure, keep a short source list:

```
Name:
  - [A] Memoir title, year, chapter/page for the claim used
  - [B] Author, *Title*, publisher, year — used for X
  - [C] Outlet, "Headline", date, URL — used for Y
  - [—] Wikipedia — entry point only, not cited
  - Confidence: high / medium / low, and why
```

Readable to the user, honest about gaps, and short enough that you actually fill
it in.

---

## 7. Honest limits

State limitations plainly when they apply:

- "I could not find a Tier A or B source for this period of her life."
- "The only detailed account is the person's own memoir, so the framing is theirs."
- "This figure is well documented for the public career and poorly documented
  for the early years."
- "I am reasoning from general knowledge here, not from retrieved sources."

An acknowledged gap costs the analysis nothing. A confident fabrication costs it
everything.
